About the Workshop

The Challenge: Robotics remains data-limited, while robot learning moves toward ever larger models
The Question: Is data all we need, or can physics priors complement limited data?
The Approach: A critical examination of the evidence for and against physics priors
The Format: Five invited talks, paper spotlights, question-driven posters, and a round-table debate

Data-driven learning is transforming robotics, but unlike language and vision, robotics remains fundamentally data-limited: collecting physical interaction data requires hardware, time, and supervision, and safety limits exploration. At the same time, robot learning is moving toward ever larger, general-purpose models, and substantial resources are being committed to pure data scaling. This raises a fundamental and timely question:

Is data all we need, or can physics priors complement limited data for robot learning?

Rather than advocating for physics-informed robot learning, the workshop will critically examine the evidence for and against it. Since the impact of physics priors cannot be assessed by a single metric across methods and tasks, every invited speaker and paper author will address the same three questions:

(Q1) Embedded physics priors. Which physical knowledge, if any, does the method exploit, and at which level of the learning pipeline: inputs and data generation, model architecture, and/or training loss function?
(Q2) Comparative evidence. Where controlled comparisons are feasible, how does the method perform relative to alternatives that use more or less physical knowledge, under comparable data and computational budgets (e.g., in terms of sample efficiency, generalization, and safety)? Otherwise, what evidence supports the claimed benefits, and what limitations prevent stronger conclusions?
(Q3) Limits and scaling. How does this comparison change as data scale, model capacity, or computational resources increase, and which other factors, such as the accuracy of the assumed physics priors, affect it?

Talks and poster clusters follow three settings that form one argument:

  • (S1) physics-consistent dynamics learning under data scarcity;
  • (S2) learning-based planning and control, where physics priors (e.g., dynamics models, energy, or stability properties) shape closed-loop behavior;
  • (S3) large-scale policies and world models, to examine the role of physics at scale.

The answers to Q1–Q3, the disagreements and open questions raised at the poster session, and the round-table conclusions will be collected in a position paper, published online after the workshop. The paper will distinguish findings supported by comparative empirical evidence, context-dependent conclusions and methodological trade-offs, and open questions where the available evidence is insufficient.

The workshop builds on a recent survey paper by members of the organizing team (arXiv:2609.22319, GitHub repository).

Question-Driven Poster Session

Accepted posters are grouped by setting (S1–S3), and each cluster, led by one invited speaker, identifies one key disagreement and one unresolved research question. These become inputs to the round table and the position paper. The assignment of speakers to poster clusters will be announced here in advance.

Round Table: Scaling Robot Learning: How Much Do Physics Priors Matter?

Moderated by Mattia Piccinini, with the five invited speakers and one early-career author among the shortlisted papers. The one-hour debate builds in three stages:

  1. What does physics buy when data are scarce? Physics can constrain what a model predicts or how a policy behaves. Which physics priors remain essential when robots move from the lab to the market?
  2. Does scale make physics unnecessary, or move it? At scale, physics may leave the model architecture and reappear in the data, or in physics-informed models that AI agents build and correct from real-world failures.
  3. Where should the community invest? The disagreements from the poster clusters are presented first. Then, each panelist states one bet for the next five years, challenged by audience questions collected via Slido. The session closes with a final vote on the opening poll.

Attendees can post questions and objections on Slido, also anonymously. The talks and the round table will be recorded and made available online.

Call for Papers

We invite papers that address the questions Q1–Q3 above, in any of the three settings:

  • (S1) Dynamics learning under data scarcity: physics-consistent, physics-informed, or physics-encoded models of robot dynamics;
  • (S2) Learning-based planning and control: dynamics models, energy, or stability properties that shape closed-loop behavior;
  • (S3) Large-scale policies and world models: the role of physics in data generation, foundation models, and learning at scale.

We explicitly welcome unconventional views and negative results, including results showing that physics priors do not help, as well as preliminary work.

Submission Guidelines

  • Papers must follow the IEEE ICRA format and have 4–8 pages (excluding references).
  • Each submission must include a short Q1–Q3 statement (about 150 words) summarizing the physics priors used, the evidence for or against them, and their limits as data and models scale.
  • Papers are non-archival: they will be posted on the workshop website and will not be part of the ICRA proceedings.
  • Submission link: coming soon.

Review and Awards

Each submission will receive at least two reviews. Besides quality, originality, and relevance to Q1–Q3, reviewers will value papers that provide clear evidence for or against the usefulness of physics priors, for example through comparative evaluations, a discussion of limitations, or results showing that physics does not help, and that are likely to stimulate discussion.

All accepted papers will be presented as posters. Based on the reviews, 5 shortlisted papers will be selected for 3-minute spotlight talks, and the author of one of them will join the round table. A Best Paper Award will be assigned by the program committee based on the reviews and the spotlight presentations.
